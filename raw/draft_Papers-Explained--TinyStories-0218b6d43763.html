Papers Explained: TinyStories

TinyStories is a synthetic dataset of short stories that only contain words that a typical 3 to 4-year-old usually understands, generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. It can be used to train and evaluate LMs that are much smaller than the state-of-the-art models (below 10 million total parameters), or have much simpler architectures (with only one transformer block), yet still produce fluent and consistent stories with several paragraphs that are diverse and have almost perfect grammar, and demonstrate reasoning capabilities.

Method

The idea behind the TinyStories dataset is to have a corpus that combines all the qualitative elements found in natural language, such as grammar, vocabulary, facts, and reasoning, but is also smaller, less diverse, and more restricted in terms of its content. A natural approach to achieve this is to use the fact that young children qualitatively attain the aforementioned intellectual abilities, albeit with much less exposure to language than an adult human. To this end, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 are used.

The models are instructed to produce content that only uses vocabulary that a typical 3-year-old child would understand. The content is restricted to have the format of short stories in English. In order to address the problem of creating a diverse dataset, a vocabulary consisting of about 1500 basic words, which tries to mimic the vocabulary of a typical 3–4 year-old child, separated into nouns, verbs, and adjectives, is collected. In each generation, 3 words are chosen randomly (one verb, one noun, and one adjective). The model is instructed to generate a story that somehow combines these random words into the story.

In addition, a list of possible features a story could have (such as a dialogue, a plot twist, a bad ending or a moral value) is constructed. For each story, a random subset of those features is generated and the model is prompted with the extra requirement for the story to have these features.

TinyStories-Instruct

To evaluate the capability of generative models in terms of instruction-following rather than just content creation, a variant of TinyStories was created in which each story is preceded by a corresponding set of instructions. The instructions are of four types:

  1. A list of words to be included in the story.
  2. A sentence that should appear somewhere in the story.
  3. A list of features (possible features: dialogue, bad ending, moral value, plot twist, foreshadowing, conflict).
  4. A short summary (1–2 lines) of the story.

For each story in the original dataset, a list of words and features that were used to create it was already available. In addition, GPT-3.5 was used to create short summaries of the stories, and from each story, a random sentence (which could be any sentence in the story except for the first one) was extracted. A random subset of these was then chosen and combined into an instruction, followed by the story itself.

GPT Eval

The model is provided with a story’s beginning, taken from a manually-prepared dataset consisting of around 50 prompts. A completion is generated using the model, and the story’s beginning together with the model’s completion are given to GPT-4, which is asked to grade the completion assignment in terms of grammar, creativity, and its consistency with the beginning of the story. To perform the full evaluation, for each of the manually-constructed prompts in the evaluation set, the trained model generates 10 completions with temperature 1. The GPT-4 evaluation scores of all the completions are averaged.

Evaluations

  • Shallower models perform better in terms of grammar compared to content consistency, meaning that model depth is more important for keeping consistent with the content than for generating syntactically correct language.
  • The score for grammar plateaus at an earlier stage than the other two scores.
  • While grammar can be mastered by relatively small models, consistency and creativity only emerge at a larger size
  • The ability to generate a completion that is consistent with the beginning of the story emerges when the hidden size of the model increases from 64 to 128.
  • The models that have only 1 layer seem to struggle quite substantially with following instructions and 2 layers seem to be sufficient for a certain extent of instruction-following.

Analysis

Models of various sizes (1M–35M parameters, 1–8 layers) are trained on the TinyStories dataset. Key analysis approaches include:

  • Generating completions for truncated stories from the training set and comparing them to original endings.
  • Using LLMs to test the quality of completions from models of different sizes and architectures.
  • Assessing models on factual, reasoning, and consistency prompts, and performance improvement with increased embedding size and layers.
  • Evaluating instruction-following and out-of-distribution generation using the TinyStories-Instruct and TinyStories-Instruct-OOD datasets.
  • Quantifying diversity using Rouge scores for word and n-gram overlap against the training set, among generated completions, and against the closest dataset story.
  • Manual inspection of generated stories to rule out memorization or template-based generation.
  • Larger models consistently generate more coherent, relevant, and creative continuations; smaller models often provide less plausible or repetitive completions.
  • Layer Count vs Embedding Dimension: Context-tracking (consistency) improves with more layers, while factual knowledge improves mainly with higher embedding dimension.
  • Models trained on TinyStories-Instruct can follow novel instructions, including combinations never seen in training, and generate coherent plots.
  • Generated completions for training prompts exhibit substantial differences from original story endings and other stories in the dataset.
  • N-gram overlap between generated texts and the dataset is very low for even small n-values, supporting novelty and creativity.
  • When using a diverse set of prompts, models produce a diverse set of completions.
  • The closest point in the dataset to a generated completion is often still far, suggesting lack of simple template matching.
