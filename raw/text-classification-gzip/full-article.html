Papers Explained: Text Classification with Gzip

The paper proposes a simple, lightweight, and universal non-parametric method for text classification that combines a lossless compressor (like gzip) with a k-nearest-neighbor classifier. This method requires no training, pre-processing, or hyperparameter tuning, achieves results competitive with non-pretrained deep neural networks on in-distribution datasets, outperforms BERT on out-of-distribution datasets (especially in low-resource and few-shot settings), and is particularly effective on easily compressible datasets.

Approach

Kolmogorov complexity K(x) is the length of the shortest binary program capable of generating a given object x. It’s a theoretical lower bound for information measurement.

To compare information content between two objects x and y, Information distance E(x, y) is defined as the length of the shortest binary program that can turn x into y. The formal definition is:

E(x, y) = max{K(x|y), K(y|x)} 
E(x, y) = K(xy) − min{K(x), K(y)}

However, Kolmogorov complexity (and hence E(x, y)) is not computable in practice

Normalized Compression Distance (NCD) is a normalized, computable version using compression algorithms to approximate Kolmogorov complexity. Instead of K(x), it uses compressed length C(x) (using a compressor like gzip).NCD is formally defined as:

NCD(x, y) = [C(xy) − min{C(x), C(y)}] / max{C(x), C(y)}

The intuition is that the more similar x and y are, the smaller C(xy) will be relative to C(x) and C(y), resulting in a smaller NCD.

Method

The method is as follows:

  1. Compress each test item x1 (C_x1).
  2. For every training item x2, compress it (C_x2).
  3. Concatenate x1 and x2, compress, and calculate C_x1x2.
  4. Compute NCD for x1 and x2: ncd = (C_x1x2 — min(C_x1, C_x2)) / max(C_x1, C_x2)
  5. Store the NCD distances; sort training samples by distance to x1.
  6. Use k-nearest-neighbors: select the top k training samples with smallest NCD.
  7. Assign the test sample x1 the label of the majority among these k neighbors.
import gzip
import numpy as np

for (x1, _) in test_set:
    Cx1 = len(gzip.compress(x1.encode()))

    distance_from_x1 = []

    for (x2, _) in training_set:
        Cx2 = len(gzip.compress(x2.encode()))

        x1x2 = " ".join([x1, x2])
        Cx1x2 = len(gzip.compress(x1x2.encode()))

        ncd = (Cx1x2 - min(Cx1, Cx2)) / max(Cx1, Cx2)

        distance_from_x1.append(ncd)

    sorted_idx = np.argsort(np.array(distance_from_x1))

    top_k_class = training_set[sorted_idx[:k], 1]

    predict_class = max(set(top_k_class), key=top_k_class.count)

Experiments

Result on In-Distributed Datasets

  • The gzip method, despite requiring no pretraining, achieved results highly competitive with more complex models, especially on medium and small datasets such as AG News, R8, and R52, often coming within a narrow margin of top performers.
  • On large datasets like YahooAnswers, gzip’s performance drops (about 7% lower than neural methods on YahooAnswers), likely due to the large vocabulary making compression more difficult.
  • BERT-based models generally performed best, maintaining robustness even with small training sample sizes. However, their advantage lessened when datasets were out-of-distribution regarding their pre-training data (e.g., SogouNews).
  • Word-based models handle large vocabulary sizes better, but their advantage is less obvious when training data is small. Both BERT and word-based models are less effective for non-English text, similar to character-based models.
  • Logistic regression with TFIDF is not the best on any dataset but shows high robustness to dataset size, performing consistently.
  • The TextLength baseline essentially performed at the level of random guessing on most datasets, except for R8 and R52, indicating that class information isn’t well reflected by text length or compressed length measures.
  • Among non-pretrained deep learning models, only HAN is regularly competitive with gzip, but unlike deep models, gzip does not require extensive training.
  • mBERT, when tested on languages it was trained on (Kinyarwanda, Kirundi, Pinyin), actually has lower accuracy than English BERT in both full-data and few-shot contexts.
  • For Filipino and Swahili, mBERT achieves much higher accuracy than BERT, especially in few-shot settings.
  • The gzip method consistently outperforms both BERT and mBERT by a large margin across all five OOD datasets, without any pre-training or fine-tuning.
  • Due to its data-type-agnostic design and the non-parametric approach gzip avoids training-induced biases.
