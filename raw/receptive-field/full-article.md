According to Wikipedia [1], the receptive field (of a biological neuron) is "the portion of the sensory space that can elicit neuronal responses, when stimulated". The sensory space can be defined in any dimension (e.g. a 2D perceived image for an eye). Simply, the neuronal response can be defined as the firing rate (i.e. number of action potentials generated by a neuron). It is related to the time dimension based on the stimuli. What is important is that it affects the received frames per second (FPS) of our visual system. It is not clear what is the exact FPS of our visual system, and it is definitely changing in different situations (i.e. when we are in danger). Wikipedia [2] says:

**Insight**: The human visual system can process 10 to 12 images per second and perceive them individually, while higher rates are perceived as motion.

The entire area an eye can see is called the field of view. The human visual system consists of millions of neurons, where each one captures different information. We define the neuron's receptive field as the patch of the total field of view — what information a single neuron has access to.

## What is the receptive field in deep learning?

Similarly, in a deep learning context, the Receptive Field (RF) is defined as the size of the region in the input that produces the feature. Basically, it is a measure of association of an output feature (of any layer) to the input region (patch).

**Insight**: The idea of receptive fields applies to local operations (i.e. convolution, pooling).

A convolutional unit only depends on a local region (patch) of the input. That's why we never refer to the RF on fully connected layers since each unit has access to all the input region.

## Why do we care about the receptive field of a convolutional network?

In image segmentation and optical flow estimation, we produce a prediction for each pixel in the input image. Ideally, we would like each output pixel of the label map to have a big receptive field, so as to ensure that no crucial information was not taken into account.

Similarly, in object detection, a small receptive field may not be able to recognize large objects. In motion-based tasks, the receptive field should be larger than the largest flow magnitude of the dataset.

Therefore, our goal is to design a convolutional model so that its RF covers the entire relevant input image region.

ImageNet classification accuracy shows a logarithmic relationship with receptive field size (Araujo et al. [3]): large receptive fields are necessary for high-level recognition tasks, but with diminishing rewards. Receptive field size alone is not the only factor contributing to improved recognition performance.

## Closed-form calculations of the receptive field for single-path networks

Araujo et al. [3] provide an intuitive way to calculate in analytical form the RF of a model. A single path literally means no skip connections in the architecture, like AlexNet.

For two sequential convolutional layers with kernel size k, stride s, receptive field r:

r₁ = s₂ × r₂ + (k₂ − s₂)

General form:

r_{(i−1)} = sᵢ × rᵢ + (kᵢ − sᵢ)

Closed form for L layers:

r₀ = Σᵢ₌₁ᴸ ((kᵢ − 1) ∏ⱼ₌₁^{i−1} sⱼ) + 1   (eq. 1)

## How can we increase the receptive field in a convolutional network?

1. Add more convolutional layers (make the network deeper)
2. Add pooling layers or higher stride convolutions (sub-sampling)
3. Depth-wise convolutions

### Add more convolutional layers

Option 1 increases the receptive field size linearly, as each extra layer increases the receptive field size by the kernel size [7]. As the theoretical receptive field increases, the effective (experimental) receptive field is reducing.

### Sub-sampling and dilated convolutions

Sub-sampling techniques like pooling increase the receptive field size multiplicatively. Sequentially placed dilated convolutions increase the RF exponentially.

Dilated convolutions introduce dilation rate r. Replace kernel size k with:

k' = r(k − 1) + 1

In (a) normal 3×3 conv with RF 3×3. In (b) 2-dilated 3×3 on layer (a) output: each element has RF 7×7. In (c) 4-dilated conv: RF 15×15. Receptive field grows exponentially while parameters grow linearly [9].

**Insight**: In deep architectures, we often introduce dilated convolutions in the last convolutional layers.

Pooling operations and dilated convolutions are effective ways to increase the receptive field size quickly [7].

Depth-wise convolutions increase RF with a small compute footprint by allowing more layers within the same parameter budget (MobileNet [10]).

## Skip-connections and receptive field

In a model without skip-connections, the receptive field is considered fixed. With n skip-residual blocks, the network utilizes 2ⁿ different paths and features can be learned with a large range of different receptive fields [8]. HighResNet [8] has maximum RF of 87 pixels from 29 unique paths, ranging 3–87 following a binomial distribution.

**Insight**: Skip-connections may provide more paths, however, based on [7], they tend to make the effective receptive field smaller.

## Receptive field and transposed convolutions, upsampling, separable convolutions, and batch normalization

- **Upsampling**: for RF computation, kernel size k=1 when doubling spatial dimensions.
- **Separable convolutions**: RF properties identical to equivalent non-separable convolution.
- **Batch normalization**: during training, receptive field is effectively the whole input image.

## Understanding the effective receptive field

Luo et al. [7] discover that not all pixels in a receptive field contribute equally to an output unit's response. The effective receptive field (ERF) defines the region containing any input pixel with non-negligible impact on that unit.

Central pixels propagate information through many paths; outer pixels have few paths. ERF resembles a 2D Gaussian distribution; non-linearities (ReLU zeroing) deviate it from Gaussian.

**Insight**: The ERF in deep convolutional networks actually grows a lot slower than we calculate in theory [7].

After training the ERF is increased, minimizing the gap between theoretical RF and ERF before training.

## Conclusion — key takeaways

- The idea of receptive fields applies to local operations.
- Design models so the receptive field covers the entire relevant input image region.
- Sequential dilated convolutions grow RF exponentially while parameters grow linearly.
- Pooling and dilated convolutions quickly increase receptive field size.
- Skip-connections provide more paths but tend to shrink the effective receptive field.
- The effective receptive field increases after training.
